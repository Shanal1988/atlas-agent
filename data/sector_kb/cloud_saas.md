# Cloud / SaaS Industry — Sector Knowledge Base

## Market Size and Growth (TAM)

Global public cloud services revenue reached $680 billion in 2024 (Gartner) and is projected to surpass $1 trillion by 2027. SaaS specifically — software delivered as a cloud subscription — represents approximately $250 billion of this total, growing at 18–20% CAGR. Infrastructure-as-a-Service (IaaS) and Platform-as-a-Service (PaaS), dominated by AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud, account for the remainder.

Enterprise software is still in the early-to-middle innings of migration from on-premises to cloud. Analyst estimates suggest 30–40% of enterprise workloads remain on-premises, representing a multi-decade migration opportunity. Vertical SaaS (industry-specific software for healthcare, construction, retail) is the highest-growth sub-segment because TAMs are greenfield — digital systems are replacing paper-based processes, not just upgrading legacy software.

## Unit Economics Framework

SaaS businesses are valued on a fundamentally different framework than traditional software or manufacturing:

**Key metrics:**
- **ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue)**: the primary revenue metric; predictable and compound-growth-oriented
- **Net Revenue Retention (NRR)**: the percentage of ARR retained from the prior cohort, after churn and expansion. NRR > 120% means the existing customer base grows on its own, before any new customer acquisition. Best-in-class: 125–145% (Snowflake, Datadog at peak growth); median SaaS: 105–115%.
- **Gross Margin**: 70–85% for pure software (no hardware or services margin drag); reflects the leverage of software economics
- **Rule of 40**: ARR growth rate + FCF margin; > 40 is considered healthy; > 60 is exceptional. Measures the trade-off between growth investment and profitability.
- **CAC Payback Period**: months of gross profit to recover customer acquisition cost. Under 18 months is efficient; over 36 months indicates a structural unit economics problem.
- **Magic Number**: net new ARR / prior quarter S&M spend. > 0.75 indicates efficient sales motion.

## Competitive Dynamics

**Winner-take-most, not winner-take-all**: SaaS markets tend toward oligopoly (2–3 dominant players per category) rather than monopoly, because large enterprise customers resist single-vendor dependence. Salesforce in CRM, Workday in HCM, ServiceNow in ITSM — each holds 25–35% market share in a well-defined category with 2–3 credible competitors.

**Platform vs. point solution tension**: Enterprise buyers face a choice between best-of-breed point solutions (higher depth, optimised for specific workflows) and platform vendors (fewer procurement relationships, better data integration, often lower total cost). The trend since 2020 has favoured platform consolidation as IT teams shrink budgets and seek fewer contracts.

**AI and the next disruption wave**: Generative AI is restructuring SaaS economics. Software copilots (GitHub Copilot, Salesforce Einstein, ServiceNow Now Assist) add 20–30% premium pricing per seat. However, AI also threatens commoditisation of basic SaaS functions — tasks that previously required purpose-built software (scheduling, basic CRM workflows, report generation) are increasingly handled by AI assistants built on top of cheaper models. Incumbent SaaS vendors with proprietary data (customer transaction history, enterprise workflow data) are better positioned to defend against this than pure-form SaaS.

## Moat Sources

1. **Workflow integration depth**: Enterprise SaaS becomes deeply embedded in daily workflows. Switching Workday HCM requires retraining 10,000 employees, rebuilding integrations with payroll and benefits providers, and migrating years of employee data. Real switching cost.
2. **Data network effects (selective)**: Platforms that aggregate cross-customer benchmarking data (e.g. Veeva in life sciences, Procore in construction) provide genuine network effects — a customer on the platform benefits from industry benchmarks generated by all other customers.
3. **Ecosystem lock-in**: Salesforce AppExchange has 5,000+ apps. SAP has 20,000+ partner integrations. The ecosystem itself becomes a switching deterrent — the customer's third-party applications are certified against the incumbent, not the challenger.
4. **Land-and-expand model**: Best-in-class SaaS companies sell a limited initial land (often a single team or department) and then expand systematically into adjacent departments and use cases. This makes the total addressable opportunity within each customer very large while keeping initial sales friction low.

## Key Risks

- **Churn acceleration in macro downturns**: SaaS contracts face scrutiny during budget cycles. Companies cancel or reduce seats of under-utilised software. NRR compresses from 120%+ to 100–105% during cost-cutting cycles, significantly reducing implied valuation.
- **AI substitution**: OpenAI, Anthropic, and the hyperscalers are building AI assistants that partially substitute point-solution SaaS. CRM data entry, basic analytics, meeting summaries, and email drafting are increasingly handled by AI layers, reducing willingness to pay for dedicated SaaS tools.
- **Hyperscaler competition**: AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud each offer native SaaS equivalents for core enterprise use cases (databases, analytics, communication tools). Bundling and preferential pricing create structural headwinds for independent vendors whose products overlap with cloud-native alternatives.
- **Valuation multiples**: SaaS commanded 20–40x ARR multiples at peak (2021). Compression to 8–15x ARR for high-growth and 4–8x for mature is now consensus. Multiples are sensitive to interest rate changes because SaaS cash flows are heavily back-loaded — DCF models penalise long-duration assets in rising rate environments.
